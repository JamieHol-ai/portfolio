## The problem

PLACEHOLDER COPY. The standard EKF linearises about the current estimate, so the
error dynamics depend on where in state space you happen to be. On a skidpad
that is fine. Under hard cornering, with heading error growing and the yaw rate
saturating, it is not — the covariance stopped describing the actual error and
the filter became confident about the wrong answer.

## Approach

PLACEHOLDER COPY. The invariant EKF puts the state on a matrix Lie group and
defines the error as a group operation rather than a vector subtraction. The
consequence that matters here is that the error dynamics become independent of
the estimate, so the linearisation stops depending on how wrong you currently
are.

> Built on wheelspeeds and IMU first, deliberately — it decouples the filter
> from the odometry's own drift, and it means adding LiDAR odometry later gives
> a before-and-after on the same dataset rather than a separate measurement
> exercise.

## What broke

PLACEHOLDER COPY. Three things, in order of how long they cost. The IMU bias
states were initialised from a stationary average that included the ten seconds
before the car actually stopped moving. The wheelspeed model assumed no slip,
which is defensible for most but not all of a run. And the group-composition
order was wrong in one place, which produced a filter that looked correct on
straight sections and drifted only in turns.

## What I'd change

PLACEHOLDER COPY. The slip model is the obvious next thing — a learned bias
correction on the IMU, trained on the same logs, is the direction I want to take
it, and it pairs directly with the filter's own bias states as a comparison.
