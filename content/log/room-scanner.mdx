## The problem

A commercial terrestrial scanner — the Topcon GLS2000 is the one I kept reading
about — will map a space out to 500 m with a resolution of about 6 mm. It also
costs between twenty and thirty thousand pounds, which puts it a long way
outside the reach of anyone who merely wants to know what one does.

So the question was whether the same idea survives being built for under £100.
Not the same range and not the same resolution, obviously, but the same
principle: sweep a beam across a room, time the returns, and end up with
something a person can recognise as the room they are standing in. The target
was a 180° field of view and a point cloud accurate enough to be read as
geometry rather than as noise.

## Hardware

The sensor is a single-point time-of-flight rangefinder — it measures one
distance along one bearing, and everything else comes from pointing it
somewhere new. A pan/tilt unit built from two hobby servos does the pointing,
and an Arduino Uno drives the whole thing.

  note="The wooden base was not in the original plan — the kit's own base was too narrow to stay still while the servos accelerated."
  caption="Fig. 1 — the assembled rig: rangefinder on the pan/tilt bracket, Arduino and servo driver alongside"
/>

Two decisions here mattered more than they looked like they would. The servos
run from a separately regulated 5 V supply through a PCA9685 driver rather than
off the Arduino's own rail — before that, unregulated mains-derived power made
them move jerkily and unpredictably. And the pan/tilt kit's base was too narrow
to be stable under its own movement, so the whole assembly is bolted to a
wooden frame I made for it. A scanner that shifts slightly each time it
accelerates is a scanner whose coordinate frame is a fiction.

The rangefinder also sits 5 cm off the centre of rotation, which has to be
corrected for in software. It is a small number that is wrong everywhere at
once.

## Approach

The loop is deliberately simple. The servos step to the next bearing, the
sensor takes a single reading, and the Arduino writes distance, return
strength, pan angle and tilt angle to the serial port. The host reads that,
converts to Cartesian coordinates, and plots the point live so you can watch the
room appear.

Each sample is a point in spherical coordinates about the scanner, so the
conversion is the one piece of real mathematics in the pipeline:

Points are coloured either by distance or by return strength, switchable after
the scan, which turned out to matter — the two colourings answer different
questions, and the strength one answers a question I had not thought to ask.

## What broke

The worst bug was a timing bug, and it is the one I would still describe first
in an interview.

The rangefinder was running in continuous mode, free-running at its own rate and
streaming readings at the serial port as fast as it could. The Arduino read
whatever was in the buffer. But the buffer was filling faster than it was being
drained, so what came out was not the current distance — it was a distance from
roughly seven iterations earlier, paired with the *current* pan and tilt angles.
Every point was being placed at the wrong bearing by a fixed lag.

What makes it a good bug is that it is invisible if you only ever sweep one way.
A constant lag on a monotonic sweep just shifts the whole cloud slightly, and the
room still looks like a room. It only appears on the return sweep, where the lag
now displaces points in the opposite direction: surfaces that should have landed
in the same place on both passes instead spread out into two separate planes.

<Figure
  src="/room-scanner/time-lag.png"
  alt="Point cloud showing points that should be coincident smeared across a plane"
  note="Every point here is a measurement of the same surface. The spread is the lag, not the sensor."
  caption="Fig. 2 — the same surface measured on the forward and return sweep"
/>

The fix was to stop letting the sensor free-run: switching it to single-ping
mode, where it measures only when explicitly commanded, means a reading is
requested, taken and consumed before the next bearing is commanded. The
association between measurement and pose becomes correct by construction rather
than by luck.

This is a sensor time-synchronisation problem, which I did not have the
vocabulary for at the time. It is the same class of failure as anything else
where two data streams are fused on the assumption that samples with the same
index happened at the same moment — and it is the reason I now treat that
assumption as something to be checked rather than relied upon.

Three others cost real time:

The spherical-to-Cartesian conversion was, for an embarrassing while, the 2D
formula. It produces results that are plausible in a plane and wrong everywhere
else, which is the worst kind of wrong.

The servos were not reaching a full 180°, because the default minimum and
maximum pulse widths did not match the HS-422s I was using. Debugging this
by watching a rangefinder is hopeless, since you cannot see where it is aimed —
so I swapped the sensor for a low-power laser pointer and calibrated the pulse
limits against something visible.

<Figure
  src="/room-scanner/laser-calibration.jpg"
  alt="Laser beams from the scanner tracing across a room to a wooden target"
  caption="Fig. 3 — calibrating the pan limits against a visible beam"
/>

And the motors juddered intermittently until I noticed the sensor and the servos
were running at different baud rates — the sensor at 115200, the servos at 9600.
Dropping the sensor to 9600 fixed it and cost nothing, because in single-ping
mode it was no longer trying to stream.

The distance colouring shows the geometry. The strength colouring shows the
material, which I had not expected to be legible at all: hard surfaces such as
wood return a strong signal, soft ones such as fabric and carpet return a weak
one. Furniture separates from walls in a way it simply does not in the distance
view. It follows directly from how the beam reflects — a rough surface scatters
some light back towards the sensor from almost any angle, a soft one absorbs
much more of it — but seeing it fall out of the data was the most satisfying
part of the project.

I ended up treating returns below a strength of 100 as unreliable and flagging
them separately rather than silently plotting them.

## Where it fell down

Three things limit this scanner, and I went after each of them with an
experiment rather than an assumption.

**Mirrors break it completely.** The sensor assumes light travels in a straight
line, so it has no way to represent a beam that has bounced. What actually
happens is that the beam reflects off the mirror, carries on to some other
object, and returns by the same path — and the scanner places that object at the
total path length, along the original bearing. The result is a phantom: a copy
of the ceiling apparently floating behind the wall.

<Figure
  src="/room-scanner/mirror-artefact.jpg"
  alt="Point cloud showing a spray of spurious points extending beyond the room's wall"
  note="The points streaming off to the upper right are a reflection of the ceiling, placed behind the mirror."
  caption="Fig. 7 — an artefact produced by a large mirror"
/>

I set this up deliberately to confirm it: a target 50 cm away, viewed via a
mirror, reads as 100 cm. The reflectivity is not marginal either — the aluminium,
silver and gold backings used in ordinary mirrors are all more than 85 %
reflective at infrared wavelengths, so essentially the entire beam survives the
bounce. Glass, tested the same way, does the opposite and is effectively
invisible: the sensor reads straight through it at any angle, which means a
window is not a surface as far as this scanner is concerned.

**The beam is not a line.** It diverges at a 3.6° receiving angle, so the spot
is about 6 cm across at 1 m and around 72 cm at 12 m. When two surfaces at
different distances fall inside one spot, the sensor does not report either —
it reports a value somewhere between them. Every edge in the point cloud is
therefore slightly invented.

I measured this rather than taking the datasheet at its word, closing two boxes
in towards the beam from either side until the reported distance changed.

<Figure
  src="/room-scanner/beam-width.png"
  alt="Graph of measured distance against beam width at 0.5 m and 1 m, with theoretical values marked"
  note="The manufacturer's figure sits at the inflection point of both curves rather than at either extreme — defensible, but it describes where the beam starts to matter, not where it ends."
  caption="Fig. 8 — measured beam width against the manufacturer's specification"
/>

**Resolution is bought with time.** A slower sweep means finer servo steps and
more samples to average, so scan quality and scan duration trade off against
each other directly. There is also a geometric limit that no amount of patience
fixes: because the scanner sweeps in angle, points spread apart with distance,
and a far wall is always sampled more sparsely than a near one.

On accuracy, the datasheet claims ±0.5 cm and a 10 cm minimum range. The
tolerance held up in testing. The minimum did not, quite — the sensor will
return readings down to about 4 cm, but roughly one in ten of them is wrong, so
I kept to the specified minimum and made the questionable points something the
user opts in to seeing.

## What I'd change

The angular resolution is the binding constraint, and hobby servos are the
reason. A geared stepper with a 0.067° step would be roughly twenty times
finer — the servos here are reliable to 1°, and merely willing to attempt 0.8°.
Better still would be to stop sweeping a single-point sensor altogether and
mount a spinning 360° LiDAR module on one servo, which collects a whole
horizontal ring per revolution and would cut the scan time enormously while
covering the full circle rather than half of it. The breadboard should be a PCB.

The thing this taught me that transferred directly is that a range sensor's
error model is not a single number — it depends on the surface, the incidence
angle, and how much of the beam lands on one thing. A pipeline that treats every
return as equally good will produce a cloud that looks plausible and measures
badly.
