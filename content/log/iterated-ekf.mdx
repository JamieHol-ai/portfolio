## The problem

PLACEHOLDER COPY. The EKF's measurement update linearises the observation model
once, about the prior mean, and then applies a correction that can land a long
way from that point. That is a bet that the posterior sits close to where the
prior started. On a skidpad it does, and the bet is free.

Under hard cornering it stopped being free. The body-frame velocity has to be
rotated into the world frame, so heading enters the model through a sine and a
cosine — and heading is exactly the state with the most uncertainty when the
yaw rate is saturating and the no-slip wheelspeed model is furthest from true.
A large innovation arriving while heading uncertainty is wide is the worst case
for a one-shot Jacobian: the update is computed with a linearisation that is
already stale by the time it is applied, and the resulting estimate is biased in
a direction the covariance does not know about.

## Approach

PLACEHOLDER COPY. The iterated EKF applies the same update repeatedly, each
pass relinearising the measurement Jacobian about the current best estimate
rather than about the prior.

<Readout

    { label: "Jacobian", value: "Hᵢ evaluated at xᵢ, not at the prior x̂⁻" },
    { label: "Terminate", value: "step norm below ε, or the iteration cap" },
  ]}
/>

Written that way the structure is a familiar one: this is Gauss-Newton on the
maximum a posteriori objective, with the prior as one residual block and the
measurement as the other, each weighted by its own covariance. The filter is
not doing anything exotic — it is solving the same least-squares problem the
EKF already solves, just without stopping after a single linearisation.

The correction term subtracted inside the bracket is the part that is easy to
leave out and expensive to leave out. It accounts for the iterate having moved
away from the prior; without it each pass applies the measurement from scratch
instead of refining one update, and the filter converges to an estimate that has
counted the same observation several times over.

The covariance is formed once, at the converged linearisation point, not once
per pass. Where the model is linear in the state the iteration terminates after
one step and the filter is exactly the EKF, which is a useful property: it
costs nothing on the straights and only does work where the nonlinearity is
actually biting.

> Built on wheelspeeds and IMU first, deliberately — it decouples the filter
> from the odometry's own drift, and it means adding LiDAR odometry later gives
> a before-and-after on the same dataset rather than a separate measurement
> exercise.

## What broke

PLACEHOLDER COPY. Three things, in order of how long they cost. The IMU bias
states were initialised from a stationary average that included the ten seconds
before the car actually stopped moving.

The second was the missing prior-correction term described above. It produced a
filter that looked *better* than the EKF on every metric that averages over a
lap, because an over-confident estimate that happens to be near the truth scores
well — and was visibly wrong the moment the covariance was checked against the
actual error.

The third was convergence. Gauss-Newton is not globally convergent, and an
update that is badly conditioned — heading nearly unobservable, wheelspeeds
disagreeing under lock-up — does not fail loudly. It iterates to the cap and
returns something confident. The filter now terminates on the step norm falling
below a threshold, caps the iteration count so the update fits inside the
control period regardless, and treats hitting that cap as a signal to be logged
rather than a normal outcome.

## What I'd change

PLACEHOLDER COPY. Damping is the obvious next thing — Levenberg-Marquardt on the
inner loop would turn the badly-conditioned cases from "iterates to the cap" into
"takes a shorter step", which is the behaviour I actually want there.

Past that, the slip model is what limits the whole thing: a learned bias
correction on the IMU, trained on the same logs, is the direction I want to take
it, and it pairs directly with the filter's own bias states as a comparison.
